Same badge tap, two different reasons to leave.

A badge log records when you arrived and when you left. It cannot record whether you had a desk. I built a made-up company with 500 people and 300 desks to see if the logs alone could tell the two stories apart. They can, if you watch for the moment the building gets too full.

A badge log cannot say why someone left early

Plenty of companies have ordered people back to the office, and plenty of people found a workaround: tap in, grab a coffee, be seen, and go home to do the actual work. It is called coffee badging. When Owl Labs surveyed hybrid workers in 2023, 58% admitted to doing it.

Some employers reached for surveillance. In August 2025 Samsung began tracking office attendance for some US staff in its semiconductor business, and the move was reported as a way to stop coffee badging.

They chose to go.They never meant to stay. The badge was the whole point.
They had nowhere to sit.They came in, found no desk, and went home to work.

The second story is real too. In January 2025, some Amazon workers reported too few desks and no parking when the company's return-to-office rule started. In September 2026, Money.ca reported that Canada's federal government could not yet seat about 34,000 employees for four days a week, under a rule that began on July 6, 2026.

Look at the badge reader and the two cases are identical: in, then out. So I built a company where I know which is which, and asked whether an analyst with only the logs could work it out.

Sources: CFO.com on the Owl Labs survey, Fast Company and HR Grapevine on Samsung, Slashdot (citing the New York Post) on Amazon, and Money.ca on Canada.

Meet Vanguard Tech

Five hundred employees share 300 desks. On Monday, June 1, 2026 the company starts a five-day office rule, and I follow the next 12 weeks. Inside the company, leaving soon after you badge in is called ghost badging. Outside, it is coffee badging.

  • Employees500
  • Desk bookings accepted (about 10% over capacity)about 330
  • Desks300
  • Desks in weeks 7 and 8, after 60 go out of service240

The booking system oversells on purpose, because some people never turn up. That is how a floor can end up with more people booked than desks. Three more things keep the story honest:

  • Part of floor 4 closes for two weeks, taking 60 desks with it.
  • Three one-off days happen: a fire drill, a company offsite, and a day the badge reader fault loses many exit taps.
  • Everyone in the company is synthetic. The names come from a fake-name library and the analysis never reads them.

Look for the hinge

Some people leave early on any day, because they want to. In the real results that floor is 13.1%, and it barely moves from an almost empty building up to about 80% full. If a crowded floor pushes people out, the rate should hold at that floor and then bend upward as the building nears capacity. Everything above the floor is the crowd's doing.

I fit exactly that shape to 19,300 person-days: a flat base, plus a ramp of 46.2 extra points that starts at 82% full and is 15 points wide. The middle of that ramp, 89.5% full, is the hinge, with a plausible range of 89.0 to 90.5%. Overall, 22.9% of person-days were short visits, meaning under 90 minutes. Past 100% full the rate sits between 56% and 63%.

Across the whole 12 weeks, about 43% of short visits (range 37 to 47%) were pushed out by a full floor.

Three ideas that decided what to measure

Person-environment fit
People stay engaged when the workplace gives them what the work needs. A missing desk is a gap in the most basic need on that list.
Job Demands-Resources, from Arnold Bakker and Evangelia Demerouti
A rule that demands presence but doesn't supply the means to work pushes people to withdraw. A desk is about as basic as a resource gets.
Psychological reactance, from Jack Brehm
When a rule takes a freedom away, people tend to find small ways to take some back. Coffee badging can be one of them.
A word on these. They shaped which numbers I collected. This data does not prove them, and I built the crowding effect in myself, so it could not.

Built in SQL, tested, then graded

Most data projects are a few CSV files and a notebook. This one keeps everything in a single SQLite database, vanguard.db, and does the cleaning in SQL, so every step can be read and re-run.

  1. Simulate the 12 weeksAbout 42,000 badge taps, 20,000 desk bookings and 500 employees go into raw tables.
  2. Pair each tap in with its tap outThe SQL fixes upper and lower case, trims stray spaces, removes double taps and handles missing taps.
  3. Count the building every 15 minutesOccupancy at each quarter hour, plus a summary for each day.
  4. One row per person per dayEach row says whether it was a short visit (under 90 minutes) and how full the building was at arrival.
  5. Run two modelsOne looks for the hinge. The other, an Isolation Forest, looks for the strangest days.
  6. Test and publishFifteen tests check the SQL matching and the models, and a Streamlit dashboard shows the results.
Nobody gets singled out. The analysis never picks out a person. Its results are counts, and the strange-days model looks at days, not people. The dashboard's data previews use synthetic records, and every name in them is fake. That is a design rule, not an accident.

How close did it get?

Because the data is made up, the true answers are known. They sit in a separate table the analysis never reads while it works. At the very end the script compares its own answers with that truth.

The hinge lands inside its range. The pushed-out share does not: the truth sits just above it. In the simulation's own books, 2,214 of the 4,429 short visits were forced by a full floor and 2,205 were chosen, with 10 for other reasons. The model counted a little under that, so I read 42.7% as a careful minimum. When the floor is full, some people who would have left anyway get pushed out first, and the method cannot tell them apart from everyone else.

Finding the strange days without being told

The company has 60 working days, shown below as 12 weeks. Thirteen of them have a note in the building log: the offsite, the fire drill, the reader fault, and the closure days. The Isolation Forest never saw that log. It flagged 9 days, and every one of the 9 has a note, so there were no false alarms. It found 9 of the 13 noted days. Tap any day to see what the model noticed.

4 noted in the log but not flagged

All three one-off days were caught. The four misses are all closure days, and their strangeness scores (0.42 to 0.51) sat just under the lowest score that got flagged (0.52). The model saw the closure days as unusual, but for these four, not unusual enough to cross the line.

Strangeness is a score from the model, higher meaning less like a usual day of that weekday. If scikit-learn is missing, the script falls back to a small NumPy version of the same idea, so it also runs on a phone.

Try the dashboard yourself

This is the live app. Use the sidebar to open Replay a Day, where you can watch one day fill up, and Desk Planner, which shows how many desks it would take to keep early leaving low.

Free Streamlit apps go to sleep when nobody visits. If the frame shows a wake up button, tap it and give it a few seconds. You can also open the dashboard on its own page.

Before quoting a number

  • The crowd effect is built in. This shows the method works. It does not show that real offices behave this way.
  • Quick visits lose their exit taps more often. That makes the real short-visit rate probably a little higher than measured.
  • Crowding and time of day travel together. The floor is calm early and busy later, so fullness and arrival time are tangled.
  • A missing exit tap is assumed to mean an 8-hour stay when counting people inside. That is a guess.
  • The psychology is a design guide. The ideas shaped what I measured. None of them is tested here.
